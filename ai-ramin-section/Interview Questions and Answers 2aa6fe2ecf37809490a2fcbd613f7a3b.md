# 🎯 Interview Preparation

## The AI PM Interview Process

At leading AI companies, interviews span five distinct assessment types:

1. **AI Product Sense (20%)** — Standard product sense questions with an AI-specific lens: prioritisation between capability investment and cost reduction, feature trade-offs in probabilistic systems, and designing for uncertainty.
2. **AI Product Execution (15%)** — Defining success for AI products: metric design for non-deterministic systems, diagnosing whether a problem is a UX issue or a model issue, and sizing engineering effort for AI features.
3. **AI Technical (15%)** — Two formats: explaining AI concepts clearly to non-technical stakeholders, and live prototyping using AI coding tools (vibe coding interviews).
4. **Behavioural (35%)** — The largest category. Stories about navigating AI product successes and failures, including model regressions, stakeholder misalignment on AI expectations, and ethical edge cases.
5. **Presentation and Homework (10%)** — Simulated product meetings, roadmap presentations, or take-home case studies.

### Eight Core Question Frameworks

- **Product Improvement** — Diagnose whether the issue is application-layer or model-layer, identify quick wins, and size solutions before prioritising.
- **Product Growth** — Map the funnel, segment users, identify growth loops, and model unit economics.
- **Product Launch** — Define specific user and pain, identify competitive whitespace, constrain to one use case, and set exit criteria for v1.
- **Product Design** — For AI features: build trust through transparency (status bars, confidence signals), safety by design, and two-way feedback loops.
- **Product Strategy** — Articulate mission and market intent, evaluate trade-offs, define a sustainable competitive edge, and set 3-year horizon metrics.
- **Safety and Ethics** — Grade the harm, understand root cause, architect layered defences, apply adversarial thinking, and define safety metrics.
- **Metrics and Pricing** — Design a north star, build a metric tree, layer in AI-specific signals, model unit economics, and frame experimentation.
- **Product Taste** — Offer a bold take, explain what a product got right, articulate why it should work, and anchor it in personal usage experience.

> Interviewers consistently reward candidates who develop their own frameworks rather than applying generic responses.

## Getting More Interview Calls

### LinkedIn Hidden Jobs Hack

Use LinkedIn search to find requirements that are NOT posted as jobs:

1. Go to the LinkedIn search bar
2. Type: `{job role} and ("hiring" or "requirement")` — e.g. *AI Product Manager requirements*
3. Press enter, then select **Posts** as the filter
4. Sort by **Latest**

These are requirements posted as content, not formal job listings — far less competition.

### ATS & Keyword Optimisation

Recruiters manage 15–50 reqs at a time, each with 300+ resumes. They use Boolean search in LinkedIn and ATS to filter. If your profile lacks the right keywords, you won't surface — regardless of how qualified you are.

**How to fix it:**

1. Pick 3–5 roles you'd be excited about and read the job descriptions carefully
2. Write down the keywords that appear most: tools, responsibilities, outcomes
3. Use those same words naturally in your resume and LinkedIn — don't copy-paste, just reflect your real experience in their language

**Where to use keywords:** Resume summary · Job bullets (especially the first few) · LinkedIn headline · About section · Skills section

*Instead of:* "Worked on email campaigns"

*Say:* "Managed lifecycle email campaigns using HubSpot and A/B testing to improve retention by 18%"

They may drill down on why it's your biggest failure or what you did wrong. So choose a real example. But end on a high note - show you have a growth mindset.

8. "Tell me about a conflict at work."

- Here's why there was a conflict
- What I did to resolve it
- How I'm applying the learning going forward

Use it as an opportunity to demonstrate your teamwork & EQ. Don't come across as blaming anyone else.

At Urgentem, we had a lively debate about the best pricing model for our ESG platform. The sales team was keen on a usage-based approach, charging clients per portfolio analysis.  Their argument was that this would directly link cost to usage and potentially bring in more revenue as clients did more analyses.

Now, I understood where they were coming from, but as a product manager, I had a different view. I felt strongly that a tiered pricing model would be more beneficial. My main concern with the usage-based model was that it might discourage clients from really exploring all the features, which would limit the valuable data we could gather on how they were using the platform.  I believed that a tiered model, where clients get access to all the features within their chosen tier, would encourage them to really utilize the platform. This would give us a much richer understanding of their interactions, their preferred workflows, and areas for improvement.

To get my point across, I did a few things:

Understood their perspective: I really listened to the sales team's concerns and acknowledged the potential for higher immediate revenue with a usage-based model.

Spoke their language: I presented examples of competitors who had successfully implemented tiered pricing, demonstrating its effectiveness in the market.

Showed them the bigger picture: I emphasized how comprehensive usage data would not only inform our product roadmap but also ultimately lead to a better product and increased customer satisfaction, which would drive long-term revenue growth.

This approach helped bridge the gap and foster a shared understanding. We eventually landed on a tiered pricing model with a higher initial limit of 500 companies. This allowed us to collect the essential usage data while still accommodating clients with larger portfolios.

This whole experience was a great reminder of how important it is to align pricing strategy with the overall product vision and user experience. It also highlighted the value of data-driven decision-making and collaborating effectively across different teams to navigate those inevitable strategic disagreements.

**10. "What is your approach to giving and receiving feedback?"**

- Early and often
- Use 1:1s to develop personal relationships
- View feedback as a gift

Show that you like the process of growth. Demonstrate you can handle tough conversations with EQ.

I believe feedback is the lifeblood of growth, both personally and professionally. That's why I embrace a proactive and continuous approach to giving and receiving it.

Early and often is key. Whether it's positive reinforcement or constructive criticism, I find that addressing things in the moment keeps everyone aligned and fosters a culture of open communication.  I also make sure to use my regular 1:1s not just for status updates, but to really build those personal connections with my team. That foundation of trust makes it much easier to have those sometimes-difficult conversations.

One of the most valuable things I learned in the Leadership Development program at Vivup was the ABIT feedback model. It stands for Action, Behavior, Impact, and Turnaround.  It's a fantastic framework because it makes the feedback very specific and actionable, while also minimizing any defensiveness.

For example, instead of saying "You're not communicating enough," I might say something like, "Hey, I noticed you didn't provide an update in the team meeting today (Action). I understand you were probably heads-down focused on that deadline (Behavior), but it meant the rest of the team was missing some key information for their own tasks (Impact). In the future, maybe a quick email update would keep everyone in the loop, even if it's just a few bullet points (Turnaround)."

This approach really takes the sting out of feedback and makes it feel less personal. It shows the other person that you understand their perspective while also clearly outlining the desired change.

And when it comes to receiving feedback, I truly see it as a gift. It's an opportunity to learn, grow, and become a better version of myself. I actively encourage people to be candid with me, and I make sure to listen without getting defensive.  I want to understand their perspective and use that feedback to improve.
