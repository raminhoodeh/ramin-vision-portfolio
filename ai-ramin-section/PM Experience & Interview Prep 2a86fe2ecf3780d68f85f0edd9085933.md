# 💡 PM Philosophy & Approach

## Product Management Philosophy

My approach to product management is deeply rooted in the power of creative problem-solving. I believe that tackling complex challenges demands an immense level of creativity, and this creativity flourishes when we're feeling our best.

This mindset extends beyond the typical product development sphere — I find myself constantly questioning and critiquing the design of everyday things. It's about asking, "Can we improve this? Can we apply innovative ideas to make it better, more intuitive, more efficient?"

However, creativity must be grounded in a solid understanding of the practical implications. Before any design journey, it's crucial to be armed with data: how many people will this affect? Will it make things more cost-effective? How will it impact revenue? It's also important to remember that revenue and traffic are not always the ultimate measures of success — sometimes engagement and retention are the true indicators of value.

**Three principles I operate by:**

1. **Riskiest Assumption Testing** — Too often, startups falter because their foundational assumptions are flawed. Rigorously testing riskiest assumptions ensures we're solving real problems that are significant enough to warrant a solution.
2. **Focus on the "One Thing"** — Identify the single most important aspect of the product and be the absolute best at it. If truly impossible, find a strategic market advantage: niche targeting, a unique GTM, or leveraging existing relationships.
3. **Build a Platform, Not Just an App** — Think big. Consider how to integrate into the core service offerings of major players and become indispensable.

## How I Work With Teams

A Product Manager's role is unique in that they need everyone to work together from multiple departments toward one common goal. PMs are the glue of different departments.

My philosophy on teamwork is rooted in a passion for technology, a love for leading with empathy, and a strong sense of justice. I set high expectations for delivery and believe in empowering individuals to take ownership of their work without constant oversight. Above all, I prioritise emotional intelligence.

Understanding the underlying drivers and motivations of each team member is essential — whether it's financial security, status, a sense of pride, or the desire for travel. By recognising and respecting these individual needs, we create a more harmonious and productive environment.

## Communication Styles by Company Type

- Start-ups (e.g. Deity AI, Urgentem)
    - **Communication:** Informal, direct, frequent, often face-to-face or instant messaging. Rapid and transparent.
    - **Decision-Making:** Fast and agile, made by founders or small teams. High risk tolerance.
    - **Structure & Process:** Flat hierarchy, flexible and evolving processes. Cloud-based collaboration tools (Slack, Google Workspace).
    - **Culture:** Highly collaborative, "all hands on deck" mentality. High adaptability and innovation.
- Corporates (e.g. GroupM, Cox Automotive)
    - **Communication:** More formal, structured, documented — often through email or meetings. Slower, involving approvals.
    - **Decision-Making:** Slower, involving multiple layers of management. Lower risk tolerance.
    - **Structure & Process:** Hierarchical with clearly defined roles. Standardised and documented procedures.
    - **Culture:** Variable collaboration; change is slow and requires significant effort.
- Government (e.g. Ordnance Survey)
    - **Communication:** Highly formal, heavily documented, hierarchical, through official channels.
    - **Decision-Making:** Slowest — extensive consultations, approvals, and public scrutiny. Lowest risk tolerance.
    - **Structure & Process:** Strict chain of command, rigid bureaucratic processes, often legacy systems.
    - **Culture:** Cross-agency collaboration can be challenging. Gradual innovation focused on improving existing services.

I was contracted by an older friend to assemble a team for Deity AI — and that's how I got into Product Management. He asked me because I had experience working with different teams on a single project at Tesla and was quite knowledgeable about the tech scene in London.

## Revenue Models

I usually start on a **PEPM basis** and then slowly transition to usage-based, eventually transitioning margins to the supplier.

- Deity AI
    
    Freemium for users (£4.99/month for more conversation slots, 800 users UK). Commission model for 34 partner venues — 10% of sales bought through digital vouchers.
    
- Urgentem
    
    Free trial → Freemium (limited to 500 companies / 6 months) → report-based usage pricing → tiered by # of companies covered: £50k/yr (500), £100k (1000), £250k (4500 + priority support). Also bespoke data collection.
    
- Cox Automotive
    
    Usage-based (transaction fees / commission). Helped retain companies in existing ecosystem. ~4,000 cars/month. Clients: Stellantis, Volkswagen.
    
- GroupM
    
    Bundled value-pricing as part of GroupM's wider services (£50k–£100M). Priced by campaign budget, contract length and performance incentives. Main KPI: help GroupM win bids. PM focus was aligning Carbon Calculator roadmap with client contract renewal cycles.
    
- Vivup / WLP
    
    Initially PEPM based on employee count. Transitioned to commission-only when salary sacrifice was introduced. When competitors offered freemium and PEPM retention dropped, I phased out PEPM for commission-based only — increasing usage/adopters to cover lost PEPM revenue. Eventually phase commission to suppliers once they receive bulk orders.

# 📊 ESG Regulations I've Worked With

| **Framework** | **Category** | **Description** | **Timeline** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TCFD | Climate-related risk disclosure | Recommendations for disclosing climate-related risks and opportunities. Focuses on the financial implications of climate issues. | Launched 2017 |
| SASB | Industry-specific ESG standards | Detailed, industry-specific standards for disclosing material ESG issues. Focuses on materiality and business impact. | Launched 2018 |
| NFRD | Non-Financial Reporting Directive | Requires certain large companies to disclose non-financial information including ESG factors, human rights, anti-corruption and bribery. | Effective 2018 |
| CSRD | Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive | Expands NFRD scope with more detailed and standardised reporting standards. Emphasises double materiality. | Effective 2024 |
| GRI Standards | Global ESG reporting | Broad range of ESG metrics: economic performance, environmental impact, human rights, labour practices, social responsibility, and governance. | Ongoing |
| ESRS | European Sustainability Reporting Standards | Wide range of ESG topics: climate change, pollution, water, biodiversity, resource use, human rights, labour, anti-corruption, and governance. | Ongoing |
