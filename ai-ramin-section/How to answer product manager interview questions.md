# **How to answer product manager interview questions**

## **Word-for-word response examples and structure:**

In today’s piece, we’re going to break down **10 of the interview questions you’re most likely to get in a PM interview**. And then we’re going to go through responses that I have crafted to the utmost detail to stand out.

We’ll cover the most common categories of PM interview questions:

1. Behavioral \- General  
2. Behavioral \- PM Specific  
3. Product Sense  
4. Product Design  
5. Product Execution

These aren’t going to be long case questions \- but instead common “behavioral-style” questions you receive. After the 10 examples, we’ll end with the 3 key takeaways for all of these questions.

# **1\. Behavioral \- General**

### **Q: What was your last set of performance feedback improvement areas?**

My last perform rating was Exceeds Expectations for my level. So none of these improvement areas were dramatic, but I was shared two main one’s:

1. The first was: empowering the team to do more. Many of the features that our team had been working on, I had intentionally involved the CEO to a great extent. But this means some individual designers and PMs felt they were being handed a roadmap.  
2. The second was: raise the bar on product reviews from my team. I tend to often be focused on the building up side of leadership, but I also need to be able to ask more of my team regularly.

I have been working on both improvement areas and have already received great feedback:

* On the empowerment side: I’ve created a clear set of big rocks to review with the CEO. We’ve reserved space on each roadmap for the individual teams. And anecdotal feedback has been quite good.  
* On the second piece of feedback, product reviews, our last product review went really well. I followed up with our Head of product and CEO, and both gave me positive feedback.

Overall, I feel like I’m making good progress on both improvement areas.

#### **What this response does well**

1. **Right length: 2 minutes**

The biggest mistake candidates make with behavioral PM questions like this is to drone on for five minutes.They would want to talk about all of the details of anecdotal feedback, for instance. *That’s the wrong approach*.

This answer take about two minutes to say, which is perfect. In interviews, we average about 100 words per minute. So in practice, focus on *200-word answers*. If the interviewer wants to go deeper, you can cue up your next 1-2 minute response once they ask it.

There’s a few well-known resources out there that have much longer examples answers. They are surefire paths to interview failure.

2. **Allays concerns**

The answer starts with the positive performance review. This starts the answer at a low temperature. And the ending about anecdotal positive feedback on improvement keeps the temperature low throughout.

The way to fail this type of question is to give the interviewer the reason to reject you. Always keep the temperature low.

3. **Truthful**

The worst thing you can do is to hide your performance feedback. Humans are predictably flawed. An astute interviewer already *knows* what type of performance feedback you would have received.

The key to succeed in this question is demonstrating that self-awareness and growth mindset. If you just say one minor thing, no one is going to believe you.

### **Q: This role will entail a good amount of stress and tight deadlines. Can you handle those?**

I hope so. At Affirm, many features I worked on were told to public markets, like Affirm Card and Split Pay in 4\. This gave the features hard deadlines and CEO visibility \- quite the mix of stress.

There are three things I focus on to make it all work. Let’s go through each.

1. First, prioritization. When it comes to prioritizing, I use the LNO framework, which stands for leveraged, neutral, overhead. I try to delegate as much neutral and overhead work as I can. I focus on leveraged tasks. This helps greatly in managing my personal stress from workload.  
2. Then the second thing I work on to control stress is self care. I love to spend time with my kids and wife, so I make time for them. That’s my form of self-care.  
3. Third, jumping into the details where needed. Tight deadlines necessitate some level of executive involvement. So for key work streams, I create weekly syncs to push forward momentum.

Overall, it’s worked well. Affirm card has 100K users from 0 6 months ago, and Split Pay in 4 is a $2B+ GMV business.

#### **What this response does well**

1. **Answers the question, while getting specific**

The challenge with this question is not being super general. Instead of falling into that trap, the response drops two specific details \- Affirm Card and Split Pay in 4\. Then it adds texture to those projects with the general answer to the question.

That’s the balance you need to strike with most behavioral questions \- bring it back to specifics. Doing both general and specific is hard to in 2 minutes/ 200 words, but this response does it.

2. **Doesn’t just answer something it can’t**

The other way people fail this question is by saying “Yes I can handle the stress” or something like that. The interviewer hasn’t really revealed what they mean here, though. So that can come off as presumptuous.

Instead, this response answers “I hope so” and explains the level of stress and tight deadlines the interviewer has had to handle. Bringing it back to the facts and specific stories you have ready to tell is the key to success in these type of generic behavioral interview questions.

# **2\. Behavioral \- PM Specific**

### **Q: Can you describe how you built your last product strategy?**

Sure, let’s talk about Self-Service at Affirm. There’s two main areas I’ll walk through:

1. Collecting key user and data insights  
2. Working with the team and iterating

So, starting with key user and data insights:

* On the user side, in talking with customers, we found that resurrected users generally came back to Affirm because we financed much higher priced products. That was our user insight.  
* On the data side, churn was really the issue. Our week 12 retention for self-service was much lower than sales: about 20%. So that was our data insight.  
* We put these data and user insights together to create our strategy: we would stay less simple, but we would offer installment payments for *much higher AOV* products.

Once I had my personal conviction based on insights, then I worked with various teams to iterate:

* The relevant teams in this case were upwards and downwards.  
* Upwards, I worked with the executive leadership team at our offsite.  
* Then, in planning, I addressed downwards: I had the product teams define their own sub-problems and initiatives within the broader umbrella.  
* This help *gain alignment* upwards teams but *empower* teams downwards.

And, in the end, the strategy worked. We moved from 75% week 12 retention to 86% over 1 year.

#### **What this response does well**

There’s a few principles from this response you should talk into all your behavioral interview questions:

1. **Mini-structure**

The easiest thing to do in interviews as a listener is get lost and lose the thread of the story. So this answer does two things to solve. First, each major area of the answer has a mini structure: Insights are broken into data and user, and strategy is broken into upwards and downwards.

Second, the respondent tells you what they’re going to say, then say it, then confirm it. This repetition nails the takeaways.

2. **Instead of telling every detail, it tells compelling details**:

*Let me let you in on a secret about interviews*. Interviewers care more about a few flashbulb memories than the actual details of everything you said.

So it’s great how the types of details this focuses on \- “about 20%” \- make it just vivid enough for the listener to remember. That’s also why saying what you said works so well.

### **Q: How do you handle disagreements with your design or engineering counterparts?**

Disagreements are inevitable \<smile\> And healthy\! Let’s talk through my high-level approach and a recent example to give it some texture.

At the high level:

* I try to make disagreement a normal and accepted part of our culture.  
* When it comes up, then I try to bring equal doses EQ and IQ to the situation.  
* From an EQ perspective, I try to make sure people are heard and feel heard.  
* Then from an IQ perspective, I try to bring it back to the facts.

This has helped me in many disagreements over mystery 15 years as a PM. A recent one I had was with my counterpart in design \- about the direction to take a big rock feature:

* She felt that we needed to slow down the design process for 3 weeks to do research.  
* I wanted to keep the pace of the time high.  
* So I scheduled a separate session to let her air out all her thoughts.  
* In that session, I asked a few questions about the root of the problem.  
* This helped incept in her a few ideas of how we could solve the problem faster.

Ultimately, we were able to run some lighter research than she had hoped for that helped us get the insights we needed. And when we launched the feature, it exceeded our north star metric’s goal by over 10%.

#### **What this response does well**

1. **Gets the “right answer”**

This is one of those questions where there’s basically a right answer. If you don’t mention elements of EQ like listening well, you’re missing the forest for the trees. This answer says the obvious things it needs to. It touches on the IQ and EQ parts of what you need to have.

And it also manages to do so in a way that does seem like platitudes. It keeps it specific and respects the question.

2. **Takes the general to specific**

These type of general questions can easily be unmemorable. You can just say the part about listening well and get the answer right \- so you won’t get rejected because of your answer. But putting yourself out there and actually explaining a specific disagreement puts your neck out on the line. And it makes you much more memorable.

Every minute in the interview where you are not making an impression is a wasted opportunity. This response seizes the opportunity to get specific to be memorable.

3. **Doesn’t get defensive or blameful**

The two ways to fail on this question are to get too defensive of your past misbehavior, or too blameful on others. This response avoids either entirely.

# **3\. Product Sense**

### **Q: What’s your favorite product and why?**

Let’s talk about software. My favorite software right now is Notion.

There are three things I’d say make that make Notion my favorite:

1. First is Notion’s Impeccable PLG chops: I’m obsessed with companies that nail the components of their product-led growth motions. Notion is the prime example of PLG. Every screen, all the permissions and user controls, it’s all perfect. One of my favorites is their onboarding, which quickly gets your job role to then customize your experience.  
2. Second is their constant innovation. Notion consistently ships big, important features \- fast. I think they brilliantly integrated AI fast recently, for instance. They probably came to market 4 weeks earlier than everyone else. So they know how to move fast. As a product leader, I love that.  
3. And the final reason Notion is my favorite software is how built for 2023 it is. Google docs is the other option that everyone has for collaborative docs, but it clearly feels like it was built for 2008\. Notion has fresh UX down to a science. In reality, much of Notion’s functionality like Search is much worse than Google. But I just like using it more.

#### **What this response does well**

1. **It shows good product sense**

The first point mentions very specific product features within the PLG category that Notion does well. It shows that the person has taste and a perspective on PLG. So in that way, it demonstrates good product sense. The rest of the answer continues to follow up with points about shipping speed and design.

The mistake people often make with this question is to not talk enough about product concepts. You should this question to show off your perspective on a variety of product topics.

2. **It sticks to a few points**

People have a tendency to get worked up over this question and share all of their excitement. But that tends to drown out delivering a few memorable points. It’s good to emphasize just 3 or so points when it comes to simple questions like this, and go a bit deeper on them in the time you have.

### **Q: How would you improve it (your favorite product from above)?**

That’s a great question. Can I take a minute to think about that?

\<After sketching out\>

Okay, so I think that the area Notion needs to work on is becoming the document home for a section of the company, like R\&D. So the goal should be to rip & replace O365 of Google Sheets for most tasks in this org. And the reason I believe so is I think this will convert the most users to paid plans, and will drive the most retention.

Notion’s top of funnel is already tremendous, so I think a strategy focused on becoming the dominant platform is key.

And what Notion is really missing, as the two key next products in those suites, is databases that have spreadsheet functionality and presentation slides.

I think that both these features don’t even need to be major departures from Notion’s existing canvases. It could just integrate the spreadsheet functionality into databases that already exist today. And it could just make slides a way to cut up and share your document.

If they pursue those integrated approaches and they succeed, then they can invest more. And I bet they can grow market share and revenue even faster than they already are.

#### **What this response does well**

1. **Takes some time**

The first thing you should do with mini-case questions like this is take some time to choose an answer that will be compelling. There’s rarely a product you’ll just know how to improve. And even if you have prepared this question, it’s helpful to write down the key points you want to hit.

This question is a “mini-case,” so it’s valuable to treat it like one.

2. **Suggests big features that have clear milestones**

The features that this response suggests are huge. But the response also includes clear MVPs of how the areas can be tested into incrementally. And if these features succeed, it would not only delight users but also grow the business. So these are great features to include.

Your interview should think, “why isn’t the product already doing that?”

3. **Clearly shows you love the product**

The worst thing you can do with this question is to choose a product you don’t actually love and isn’t software. Then when it comes to the improvement question you can’t generate a really deep response like this, with its mentions about the details of tables.

That extra layer of detail is key in product-building, and it should come off in your answer, as well.

# **4\. Product Design**

### **Q: How do you ensure a product's design meets both user needs and business goals?**

So there’s two elements to my approach.

First, the way I think about it is the corporate and business strategy ultimately comes first. What are the areas in the metrics, strategically, that the business has to move?

These become the narrowing focus for what we focus on.

So at Affirm, that was activation rate. Whatever the area that you need to focus on becomes your north star. Then, you can use that to narrow your aperture to very specific user problems.

At Affirm, users just didn’t know about our availability outside of integrated checkout. And if they did, they found the product hard to use. So those were two problem areas we spent over two years working on.

Then the second element to my approach, to make sure we really solve problems for users, is to do a ton of user testing and quick iterating.

That helps reveal really interesting edge cases. Like at Affirm, that was delinquent and ineligible users, which we then went and solve their very specific problems for.

With that combination of the business narrowing the problems, and user testing, I find my product teams succeed in balancing user needs and business goals.

#### **What this response does well**

1. **It has a general framework that goes specific**

The template for these types of questions is to answer the question generally, but infuse specifics. This response puts texture around all of the high-level points made with examples of specific business problems, user problems, and insights. Showing that you’re the type of product person who gets specific is very helpful in interviews.

2. **It doesn’t brush off the question**

Questions like this can feel almost basic if you’re an experienced PM. I’ve seen some interviewers just basically reject the question. They’ll say a four line response. That’s not the move here. You want to “Strong man” every question (assume the interviewer’s best intent).

3. **It has a clear structure**

You’d be surprised how many people just drone on for this question. Always try to structure your answer around 2-3 points.

### **Q: What's the most challenging design problem you've faced, and how did you overcome it?**

Can I take a second to think about most challenging?

\<Take a minute\>

Okay. So the most challenging design problem I’ve faced was improving the information architecture on Fortnite.

The problem for us to solve was that players, especially new players who were our main focus at the time, find the game’s control placement unintuitive.

But if we made a change to simplify the game for new users, we were going to spark a revolt amongst our hundreds of millions of existing players.

So it felt like we had a few options: we could just teach new players, we could make a change for everyone, or pull a cat out of a hat.

The team was dejected. My normally jubilant designers felt like all the other information architecture changes we had made were hated by players and this wasn’t worth working on.

So I put on my own design hat and used my own double diamond framework, and I brought the game’s lead designers along with me.

As they saw me start to execute on mockups in front of players, they got excited about the project and came back on.

Ultimately, over four seasons we shipped 20 small changes. But we slowly transformed the IA in a way that was easier for new players but acceptable for existing.

#### **What this response does well**

1. **Tells a story**

When someone asks you about your “most” anything, they’re looking for a story. They’re looking for why the situation was so exemplary, so notable. So you have to tell a story to build that up.

This response builds context and creates a problem \- the team didn’t want to do the work. But then it shows how the interviewee (the hero) solved the problem. That’s the arc you want to take for these questions.

2. **Focuses on PM insight details**

A lot of people will get overly caught up in the details of their story and forget to zoom out to PM lessons and learnings. But you are in a PM interview. So it’s best to infuse things like the double diamond framework and prioritizing what users matters (new one’s).

These show you think and act like a great PM.

# **5\. Product Execution**

### **Q: How do you manage the trade-offs between speed of execution and quality?**

I tend to think about one more element in the trade-off picture: and that’s impact.

We all want more speed, quality, and impact. But, generally, in my experience, as a product leader it’s best to push the team in one direction at any given time.

So what I would think about is: where is the team now, what direction do we go, and how do I help us get there?

Let me give an example of my time at Affirm:

* First, where is the team now? I found that the team was incredibly slow. Impact was less than desirable too, but things took ages to ship.  
* Second, what direction do we go? Given that speed was more of an issue than impact, but quality was good, I pushed the team towards speed.  
* And finally, how do I help us get there? I tried to make the team itself come to the conclusion by asking the right questions in lead syncs. They brainstormed and prioritized solutions. Then I led from the front about getting them implemented fast. We had to change the release train, the review process, and our entire philosophy.

But ultimately, we went from something like 12 weeks from idea to in production to about 6 weeks. That was a critical change we needed to make as a growth team to consistently hit our OKRs.

#### **What this response does well**

1. **Shows a level of PM theory**

Beginners take on other people’s frameworks, advanced folks generally ditch the frameworks, and experts create their own. If you show that expert level of mastery, you separate yourself from the pack as someone with a lot of PM wisdom.

Pushing back a bit on the premise of the question is the type of thing people with a point of view do, not beginners.

2. **Presents a relatable situation**

Many people have been in the situation where teams are shipping slowly. This shows that the respondent can help turn around a situation.

The place where this answer goes deepest is how they specifically enabled the team. That’s critical detail to zoom in on.

### **Q: Tell me about a product launch that didn't go as planned. What happened?**

Let’s talk about the outfits launch at thredUP.

This was right after our Series D launch. I had really helped improve conversion, so I felt a bit overconfident in my product sense.

We decided to build an outfits tool as one of the big rocks on our shopping team, which was run by one of my best PMs.

I trusted them to figure out how to make impact.

But the idea was botched from the start.

In our first set of usability tests, we didn’t get overwhelming signal.

But we still went ahead with the launch.

We had gotten marketing ready with several campaigns.

We launched \- and the metrics fell flat. Although we had decent adoption becaus we had marketing, retention was abysmal.

People used the outfits tool once and never again.

It was more a novelty than a product feature.

I had to make a call on whether we were going to invest more or just move on to the next thing. Both me and my PM agreed the data was overwhelming \- we should just move on to the next thing.

The next quarter, that shopping team had a huge impact on several totally different initiatives, because we were willing to pivot away from the bad launch.

#### **What this response does well**

1. **It takes the failure and shows what you did to flip it**

All of these questions are looking for an honest failure. But after that, they’re looking for you to solve the problem. And they want details on what you did. So that’s the area to concentrate your explanation time.

2. **Uses a storytelling framework**

Answering a bunch of interview questions in a row with a formulaic structure or framework makes you boring. Mixing in storytelling frameworks like this helps keep your interviewer engaged \- and make you seem human.

2. **It’s still on time**

That story, with all its twists and turns, was still 200 words \- or 2 minutes.

## **Three Key Takeaways**

Let’s put together the lessons from all the interview questions’s we’ve looked at today. Here are the three things you should remember walking away from this piece:

1. **Remember to take the general to the specific**: PM interviews are filled with curveball generic questions. But the way to blend in with the crowd is to answer with platitudes. Specific stories you have in your back-pocket are you secret weapon.  
2. **Structure is your friend**: Almost all of your responses should have a numbered or bullet point structure. If not, use a storytelling framework. Never go structure free.  
3. **Keep it concise**: People are always surprised looking at these answers on paper how little detail there is. It’s impossible to include much more detail in 2 minute responses.
